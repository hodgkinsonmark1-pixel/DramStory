# Brand Voice — How to Actually Write for This Site

Extracted from the original DramStory operating guide (superseded 19 July
2026 - this and `content-structure-conventions.md` between them cover
everything from that guide not already living in another doc).

- **Tone:** insider, playful, sophisticated. Celebrates *planning* as part
  of the adventure, not just the destination.
- **Write like someone who's actually been there**, not like marketing copy
  describing a place secondhand. Concrete, specific, sensory details beat
  generic superlatives every time - "the pool is heated by waste heat piped
  straight from Bowmore Distillery next door" beats "a unique local
  attraction."
- **Evocative but not overwrought.** Short paragraphs, real texture, no
  purple prose. A day's narrative should set a mood and explain *why* the
  day feels the way it does - it is not a restatement of the stop list
  underneath it. See `day-narrative-standard.md` for the fuller, Hub-Day-
  specific version of this same principle, with worked before/after
  examples.
- **Honesty over salesmanship.** If something needs booking ahead, say so
  plainly. If accommodation isn't bookable yet, say that too, rather than
  implying more polish than exists. The brand's credibility depends on this
  - it's a planning tool for people who'll actually show up at these
  places.
- **Never fabricate specific factual claims** (opening dates, prices,
  historical trivia) that aren't grounded in verified source data. General
  emotive description of a well-known, visually obvious place (e.g.
  describing a beach as beautiful) is fine; inventing unverifiable
  specifics is not.

## Voice budget by page type (8 Sep 2026)

A day plan and an About page are not the same genre and should not share a
voice budget. Utility pages are judged in two minutes by someone deciding
where to go; brand pages are read by someone deciding whether to trust us.

| Page type | Budget |
|---|---|
| Days, distilleries, tours, local features | **Tight.** State, don't perform. Every sentence earns its place against the booking decision. |
| Journal | **Middle.** Narrative allowed, one turn per piece, colour where the subject supports it. |
| About, brand and founder pages | **Loosest.** Warmth, humour, a genuine emotional peak, a first-person voice with a personality in it. |
| Social | **Widest tonal latitude, narrowest legal latitude.** See below. |

## The earning test (8 Sep 2026)

Replaces the old flat ban on rhetorical hinges, pivots, fragments and
emotive adjectives. That ban was aimed at the right fault but hit the wrong
target: it caught good writing along with bad, and the corrected drafts came
back accurate and flat, which is its own failure.

**Delete the construction and read the sentence again.**

- If it still says the same thing, the construction was decoration. Cut it.
- If the sentence collapses, it was doing work. Keep it.

So "That is the thing about Islay" is fine when a real turn follows it, and
banned when it's just throat-clearing before more of the same. "Wonderful
trip" fails the test every time - delete "wonderful" and nothing is lost.
"Roads that make distances on a map largely theoretical" passes: there is no
shorter way to say it, and it changes how someone plans their day.

### Still absolutely out, budget regardless

- **Fabricated or unsourced specifics.** Unchanged, and the reason for the
  whole sourcing standard.
- **Borrowed glamour.** "As beautiful as any Caribbean island" describes
  nothing about Islay; it asks the reader to picture somewhere else. Say
  what is actually in front of you. (A self-aware joke at the comparison's
  expense is a different thing and can stay.)
- **Adjective stacking as a substitute for a detail.** "Astonishingly
  beautiful landscapes" is weaker than one thing you actually saw.
- **Stacked verbless fragments** used for rhythm - "Warehouse floors. The
  smell of a mash tun." One fragment for emphasis is fine; three in a row is
  a tic.
- **Telling the reader how to feel** about something the page hasn't shown
  them yet.

## Social voice (8 Sep 2026)

**Precedence.** The *Marketing & Editorial Intelligence Operating Specification*
is authoritative for social voice register, and the `dramstory-social` skill
carries CAP 18, sourcing, scoring and workflow. This section is only how the
house voice sits on top of those. Where it disagrees with either, they win.

Social is the one place the writing can be at its warmest and its most
constrained at the same time. Those pull in opposite directions, and getting
it wrong in either direction is a real failure - a stiff feed and a
non-compliant one are both losses.

**Lead with place, not product.** A dunnage warehouse with the Atlantic a few
feet away is more arresting than a bottle, and sits entirely outside CAP 18.
Place, process and people before the liquid, every time. This is the rare
rule that is simultaneously the safest option and the best copy.

**The warmth trap - the single most likely breach.** *"A dram to warm you
after the Atlantic wind."* That is the sentence this account will write by
instinct, and it breaches CAP 18.7: no drink may be portrayed as changing
mood, physical condition or behaviour. Warming, restoring, steadying,
settling the nerves, earning it after a walk - all out. The dram can be
where you end up. It cannot be what fixes you.

**Do not over-correct into blandness.** Over-caution is a logged failure on
this project, not a safe default. Sport and physical activity are permitted
(only *drinking then doing it* is banned). Factual ABV is permitted.
Sociability is explicitly permitted - do not strip the people out. And
"nobody has to drive" is both compliant and the strongest thing in the
proposition.

**The caption carries the substance.** Never write a caption that points at
the cards - "swipe to see", "the answer's on slide 4". Someone reading the
caption alone should get the value. This is a reach and accessibility
matter as much as a voice one.

**Alt text is copy, not metadata.** One entry per card, written by whoever
wrote the caption, in the same voice. A text-card carousel with no alt text
contains literally nothing for a screen reader.

**Name the ferry route.** Never "the Jura ferry" - there are two, and
conflating them tells a reader the island is unreachable in winter.
Islanders spot it instantly.

**Frame closures as what is open.** Same facts, opposite valence, and it
avoids passing a verdict on someone's business.

**Seasonal register.** Autumn and early winter recruit an audience rather
than convert one: image-led, atmospheric, the island itself. January
converts. Judge autumn posts on saves and shares, not bookings.

**Small islands.** Roughly 3,000 people on Islay, 230 on Jura. Never write
anything at a local's expense, and never make a small operator's stumble
into content.
